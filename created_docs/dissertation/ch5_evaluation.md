# Chapter 5 — Testing and Evaluation

## 5.1 Evaluation Protocol

Evaluation proceeds on two levels. The software artifact is verified by automated testing (§5.2); the predictive claims are evaluated against the held-out Sri Lankan sample of 230 records containing 33 positive cases, a prevalence of 14.4% (§5.3–§5.8). The three stated objectives are then assessed individually (§5.9–§5.11) and the whole is qualified by an explicit statement of validity threats (§5.12).

Every quantitative result in this chapter is produced by a named script that writes a machine-readable report, and no figure appears here that cannot be regenerated by running it. This convention is what allows the limitations reported below to be stated with confidence rather than hedged.

## 5.2 Software Testing and Quality Assurance

The HR platform is covered by two automated backend suites — nineteen integration test files exercising the services against a live PostgreSQL instance, and a separate unit suite covering pure business logic such as statutory payroll calculation without a database dependency. Integration tests run sequentially because they share database state. The frontend is tested with component tests and headless browser end-to-end automation, with automated accessibility checking against WCAG rules. Both codebases enforce a zero-warning lint policy and strict type checking as separate gates.

Security testing is documented separately, comprising a penetration test report and a threat model, with rate limiting on authentication endpoints, security headers, environment-scoped CORS and schema validation at every endpoint.

The limitation to record is that this establishes **functional correctness, not usability**. That distinction is what §5.10 addresses, and the two should not be conflated: a system can be entirely correct and still be unusable.

## 5.3 Headline Result: Transfer versus Local

The two models defined in §3.2 were evaluated against the same Sri Lankan sample.

**Table 5.1 — Two-model comparison on the held-out Sri Lankan sample (n = 230, 33 positives)**

| Model | Features | ROC-AUC | PR-AUC |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Transfer (trained internationally) | 4 | 0.641 | 0.285 |
| Local (cross-validated within Sri Lanka) | 8 | 0.937 | 0.790 |

The headline contrast is large and it is the empirical contribution of this work. A model carrying knowledge from Saudi and Russian labour markets performs close to chance on Sri Lankan data, while a model fitted to Sri Lankan psychometric constructs performs strongly. This quantifies the research gap: **Sri Lanka needs its own HR data, and the absence of it cannot be substituted by international data on shared demographic features.**

Three qualifications must accompany that statement, and none of them is minor.

**The two figures are not like-for-like.** The transfer figure is genuine out-of-sample performance across national contexts. The local figure is cross-validated *within* the same 230 records. They answer different questions — "does knowledge transfer?" and "is there learnable signal here?" — and the gap between them is therefore not a clean experimental contrast between two competing models.

**The transfer figure is unstable.** The 0.641 headline is produced by the resampling configuration used in the training script at a single seed. Across five seeds that same configuration averages 0.718 with a standard deviation of 0.100 — ten AUC points of seed-to-seed variation. The alternative class-weighting configuration averages 0.828 with a standard deviation of 0.014. The transfer model's measured performance therefore depends more on the resampling choice and the random seed than on anything the data contains. A task with substantial signal does not swing nineteen AUC points on a preprocessing decision, and §5.5 shows what is actually driving the variation.

**The outcomes are differently defined.** As established in §3.2, the training data records observed attrition and the validation data records turnover intention. Part of the transfer model's weakness is attributable to this label shift rather than to national context, and the design cannot separate the two.

![Figure 5.1 — SHAP summary for the local model, showing per-construct contributions to predicted turnover intention.](reports/shap_local.png)

A note on the transfer model's feature importances is required. Age accounts for 0.612 of that model's importance, which the interim report described as age dominating the model. That description should not be carried forward. As §5.8 shows, age in the Sri Lankan validation data takes only four distinct coded values with 88.7% of records at a single one; it is almost certainly a set of age-bracket midpoints rather than measured ages. A feature with four levels and one dominant mode cannot support a substantive claim about age effects, and the apparent dominance is an artefact of that coding.

## 5.4 Leakage and Common-Method Bias Audit

A ROC-AUC of 0.94 on 230 records with 33 positives invites the suspicion that something has leaked. The audit tested three possibilities.

**Imputation leakage: none, and structurally impossible.** The construct data has a missing rate of exactly zero, so imputation is a no-op. Refitting with imputation confined inside each cross-validation fold reproduces the original result to four decimal places — 0.9366 either way, a difference of 0.000.

**Item overlap between predictors and target: none.** The turnover-intention composite is computed from four items that form a disjoint group from every predictor construct. Construct-to-target correlations range from −0.429 to −0.613; these reflect genuine association together with shared-method variance, but not the arithmetic self-correlation that item overlap would produce.

**Operating point: optimistic, and now corrected.** The originally reported precision of 0.73 and recall of 0.82 were obtained at a threshold selected on the same predictions being scored. Selecting the threshold nested within the training folds gives the honest figure: **precision 0.58, recall 0.879**. Recall in fact improves; precision falls by fifteen points. The nested figure is the one reported throughout this dissertation.

The 0.94 therefore survives, with an honest confidence interval. Bootstrap resampling gives a 95% interval of [0.883, 0.982], per-fold standard deviation is 0.047, and the Brier score is 0.056, indicating well-calibrated probabilities rather than merely good ranking.

What the audit **cannot** rule out is common-method bias in the strict sense [@podsakoff2003]. Predictors and outcome are collected from the same respondent, in the same instrument, at the same moment. Some portion of the 0.94 reflects consistency in how individuals answer self-report scales rather than a predictive relationship between constructs and behaviour. This is a limitation of the source instrument, not of the modelling, and it cannot be resolved without behavioural outcome data.

## 5.5 Synthetic Data Ablation

The third research question asks whether calibrated synthetic augmentation contributes, and whether the 2.0 and 0.5 sample weights are justified. Six conditions were run across five seeds.

**Table 5.2 — Synthetic-data ablation on the transfer model (mean ROC-AUC across five seeds)**

| Condition | ROC-AUC | SD |
|---|---:|---:|
| Class weighting, real 2.0 + synthetic 0.5 (current recipe) | 0.821 | 0.020 |
| Class weighting, equal weights | 0.828 | 0.014 |
| Class weighting, real data only | 0.788 | 0.031 |
| Class weighting, **synthetic only** | **0.526** | 0.103 |
| SMOTETOMEK with synthetic (headline path) | 0.718 | 0.100 |
| SMOTETOMEK, real only | 0.655 | 0.090 |

Three conclusions follow. Synthetic augmentation produces a small positive effect of +0.032 ROC-AUC under class weighting, at the edge of seed noise. The 2.0 and 0.5 weighting is **immaterial** — a difference of −0.007 against equal weights, well inside noise, so the weighting scheme is not doing the work it was assumed to do. And synthetic data alone performs at 0.526, effectively chance, confirming that it functions as augmentation and could not substitute for real data.

The more consequential finding came from decomposing the transfer signal by feature group. Age and gender together score **0.457** — below chance. Job satisfaction and work-life balance together score **0.825**, essentially the full four-feature performance of 0.828.

**Whatever the transfer model achieves comes entirely from satisfaction items, not from demographics.** And those satisfaction items are, on the Sri Lankan side, self-report collected in the same instrument as the intention outcome. The transfer model is therefore not demonstrating cross-cultural transfer of attrition knowledge; it is partly re-detecting same-source correlation between satisfaction and intention. This reframes the headline: the transfer result is weaker than 0.64 makes it appear, not stronger.

## 5.6 Estimator Justification

Random Forest was compared against logistic regression and gradient boosting on identical data, folds and seeds.

**Table 5.3 — Estimator comparison (mean ROC-AUC across five seeds)**

| Arm | Random Forest | Gradient Boosting | Logistic Regression |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Local (8 constructs) | **0.937** | 0.853 | 0.823 |
| Transfer, class weighting | 0.821 | — | **0.854** |

On the local arm Random Forest wins outright: +0.084 over the next best, a difference of 7.79 standard deviations against seed noise, and it is also the best calibrated of the three (Brier 0.061 against 0.166 for logistic regression). The estimator choice is vindicated exactly where the strong result lives.

On the transfer arm it does not win. Logistic regression is better by 0.033 and roughly ten times more stable. This is reported rather than omitted, and it corroborates §5.5: a four-feature task on which a linear model matches an ensemble is a task without much structure to find. The retention of Random Forest for the deployed local model is justified on performance; its use in the transfer arm is a consistency choice, not a performance one, and is stated as such.

## 5.7 Threshold Sensitivity

The binary label depends on a cut at 3.5 on the intention composite. If the headline contrast were an artefact of that choice, the finding would be fragile.

**Table 5.4 — Contrast at alternative binarisation cut-points**

| Cut | Positives | Local | Transfer (CW) | Contrast |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| ≥ 3.0 | 51 | 0.908 | 0.777 | +0.131 |
| ≥ 3.5 (adopted) | 33 | 0.937 | 0.821 | +0.116 |
| ≥ 4.0 | 12 | — | — | indicative only |

The contrast survives every cut tested, ranging from +0.095 to +0.218 depending on cut and resampling path. Only the 3.0 and 3.5 cuts clear the twenty-positive reliability bar adopted for this analysis; at 4.0 only twelve positive cases remain and the bootstrap interval widens accordingly, so that cut is reported as indicative and excluded from the conclusion.

One disclosure is owed: **3.5 is also where the local model performs best**, which means the adopted threshold flatters the strongest number. The contrast does not depend on it, but the specific figure of 0.937 does.

## 5.8 Fairness Audit

**Fairness cannot be fully validated on this dataset, and the reason is structural rather than an oversight.** Reporting why is more useful than reporting a table that the data cannot support.

The sample contains 73 women, of whom **2** are positive cases, and 157 men, of whom 31 are. Age takes four distinct coded values — 25, 35, 45 and 52 — with 88.7% of records at 25. A subgroup ROC-AUC computed on two positive cases is meaningless, and an age-band analysis on a variable with one dominant level is not supportable. The audit therefore refuses to compute those figures and marks the subgroups unevaluable rather than printing numbers with a caveat elsewhere.

**Table 5.5 — Subgroup performance where the data supports it**

| Group | n | Positives | ROC-AUC | Recall |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| All | 230 | 33 | 0.943 | 0.818 |
| Gender = male | 157 | 31 | 0.941 | 0.807 |
| Gender = female | 73 | 2 | unevaluable | — |
| Age = 25 | 204 | 27 | 0.943 | 0.778 |
| Age > 25 | 26 | 6 | unevaluable | — |

Where performance is evaluable it is consistent with the overall figure. Selection-rate parity was assessed against the four-fifths rule, and **both attributes fail it — but the two failures mean opposite things**, which is why the raw ratios alone would mislead.

For gender, the selection-rate ratio is 0.416, below the 0.8 threshold. The **base-rate** ratio, however, is 0.139: the underlying outcome disparity is far larger than the disparity the model produces. Women are flagged at three times their own base rate, men at one time theirs. Under differing base rates, four-fifths parity and calibration are mathematically incompatible [@kleinberg2016], [@chouldechova2017]; this is a documented trade-off to argue, not a defect to remove. For age, the selection ratio of 0.462 sits *below* the base-rate ratio of 0.574, meaning the model amplifies rather than narrows the existing disparity — a genuine flag, though on a subgroup of 26 with 6 positives and therefore indicative only.

Two further tests inform the deployment position. A proxy test asked whether gender can be recovered from the eight constructs the deployed model actually uses: cross-validated ROC-AUC **0.655** — weak but non-zero. The claim "the local model excludes protected attributes" is therefore *mostly* true and must be stated that way. And a drop-and-test on the transfer model found that removing age and gender changes performance by **+0.004** — that is, removing the protected attributes costs nothing and marginally helps, consistent with the finding in §5.5 that they score below chance alone.

**The recommendation is therefore unambiguous: age and gender should be dropped from the transfer model.** There is no accuracy argument for retaining them. Subgroup validation is treated as an explicit precondition of real deployment, not as a solved problem.

## 5.9 Objective 1 — Recall Above 80%

The target was recall above 80% on the attrition class, chosen because the costly error in early warning is the employee who leaves unflagged.

**The objective is met by the local model at its honest operating point: recall 0.879, precision 0.580.** This is the nested threshold from §5.4, not the optimistic one.

The transfer model must not be credited with meeting it. On the Sri Lankan sample it achieves recall of 1.000 at the default threshold — by flagging 86% of the workforce, at a precision of 0.167. Perfect recall obtained by flagging almost everybody is not early warning; it is noise. This is precisely why §3.8 specifies that recall is never reported alone, and it is the clearest illustration in this work of why a single metric cannot carry an evaluation.

## 5.10 Objective 2 — Usability

**The planned System Usability Scale study was not conducted.** No participants were recruited and the remaining project timeline did not permit recruitment. The objective is therefore **unmeasured — neither met nor refuted** — and is reported as such rather than quietly dropped.

**Part A — the protocol as designed.** The instrument is the standard ten-item System Usability Scale [@brooke1996], administered after task completion to five to ten participants matched to the SME HR-manager persona. Four task scenarios were specified: locate the dashboard after login; identify the three highest-risk employees; explain why a given employee is flagged; and generate a month's payroll. Responses are scored to the 0–100 composite and interpreted against established adjective bands, on which a score above 80 corresponds to "excellent" and 68 represents the industry average [@bangor2009]. The protocol is fully specified and directly repeatable by anyone continuing this work.

**Part B — heuristic evaluation.** In place of user-based measurement, the interface is assessed by expert inspection against Nielsen and Molich's ten usability heuristics [@nielsen1990], applied across the same four task scenarios, with each observation assigned a severity rating.

No findings are stated until the inspection is actually carried out; a fabricated usability table would be worse than an absent one.

Two limitations apply to this substitution regardless of outcome. Heuristic evaluation is an **inspection method, not a measurement** — it surfaces usability defects but yields no satisfaction score, so it cannot answer the "above 80" target in its own terms. And the inspection is conducted by the system's own developer, against a method whose originators recommend three to five independent evaluators. A single non-independent evaluator is a genuine weakness of this evaluation and is named as one.

## 5.11 Objective 3 — Operational Cost Below LKR 10,000 per Month

**The objective is met, with approximately 2.5 times headroom: LKR 4,050 per month on the most conservative scenario evaluated.**

As established in §3.10, project-level billing could not answer this question because the deployment shares a cloud project with four unrelated systems. Cost was therefore measured per resource over a four-month window and priced against published rates, under four scenarios.

**Table 5.6 — Attributed monthly cost against the LKR 10,000 target**

| Scenario | USD | LKR | Result |
|---|---:|---:|:--|
| Attributed share, free tier applied | 7.47 | 2,256 | Pass |
| Attributed share, free tier ignored | 7.79 | 2,353 | Pass |
| Single-tenant SME, free tier applied | 13.09 | 3,953 | Pass |
| **Single-tenant SME, free tier ignored** | **13.41** | **4,050** | **Pass** |

The headline is the last row, because it makes no use of a free-tier allowance that is granted per billing account rather than per service, and because it charges the whole database instance to this system as a dedicated deployment would.

Three findings are more interesting than the pass itself. **Compute is not the cost**: all three services total approximately LKR 82 per month and fall entirely within the free tier, and the inference service consumed **150 billable instance-seconds across four months** — scale-to-zero behaving exactly as §4.5 claims. **The always-on database dominates** at LKR 2,828 per month, roughly 70% of the total: the one component that cannot scale to zero is the one that costs money, which is the architectural lesson of the cost study. And **container image storage is a hidden line item** at LKR 1,140 per month for 37.7 GB of accumulated build artefacts — real, but largely removable with a retention policy.

Three limitations qualify the result. Unit prices are published rate-card figures rather than invoiced amounts. The apportionment in the attributed scenarios rests on an assumption about shared-database usage, which the single-tenant scenario avoids. Most importantly, **the measured usage reflects development traffic, not a live fifty-employee workload**; the measured and modelled figures are reported separately and neither should be read as the other.

## 5.12 Threats to Validity

**Construct validity.** The Sri Lankan outcome is turnover *intention*, not observed departure. Intention is an established antecedent of turnover [@griffeth2000] but the two are not equivalent, and every claim made about the local model is a claim about intention. Relatedly, predictors and outcome share a single self-report instrument, so common-method variance [@podsakoff2003] inflates the local result by an amount this design cannot quantify.

**Internal validity.** Imputation leakage and item overlap were tested and excluded (§5.4), and the operating point was corrected. The residual threat is that the local figure is a within-sample cross-validated estimate on 230 records with 33 positives; the bootstrap interval of [0.883, 0.982] is wide, and small-sample optimism cannot be fully excluded.

**External validity.** The validation sample is drawn from Sri Lankan *startup* professionals, which is not the SME population the framework targets. The training sources are Saudi and Russian, chosen for availability rather than similarity. Cost is measured on development traffic in a shared project. None of these generalises without qualification.

**Conclusion validity.** All comparisons are averaged across five seeds and reported with dispersion, and differences are stated against seed noise rather than as bare point estimates. Where the data cannot support an inference — subgroup fairness, the 4.0 cut-point — the analysis declines to compute it rather than reporting a fragile number.

**Fairness and deployment.** Subgroup performance is unvalidated for women and for employees over 25 (§5.8), and gender is partly recoverable from the deployed model's inputs at ROC-AUC 0.655. The system should not be deployed on real employees without subgroup validation on a sample that can support it.

**Artifact evidence.** The Pulse Check was implemented and functionally verified but never populated with real employee responses (§4.7); its short-form reliability is consequently unassessed, and no claim about real employee sentiment is made anywhere in this work.
