# Chapter 2 — Literature Review

This chapter surveys the five bodies of work this study draws on: machine learning for attrition prediction, explainable AI in employment decisions, the transferability of predictive models across contexts, the economics of serverless deployment, and the adoption of HR analytics in small firms and developing economies. It closes by locating the gap the study addresses. The review is organised to make one argument visible: the field has largely *assumed* that an attrition model learned in one organisational and national context carries to another, and has rarely tested that assumption directly. §2.3 develops this point, because the central empirical result of this dissertation is a test of exactly that assumption.

## 2.1 Machine Learning for Employee Attrition Prediction

Turnover research long predates machine learning. Mobley's withdrawal model [@mobley1977] and the meta-analytic consolidation by Griffeth et al. [@griffeth2000] established the antecedents that still structure the field — satisfaction, commitment, perceived alternatives, tenure — and quantified their typically modest bivariate associations with leaving. Allen [@allen2008] translated this into practitioner terms, framing turnover as a cost centre amenable to management rather than an unavoidable overhead.

The machine learning contribution has been to treat attrition as a supervised classification problem rather than a hypothesis-testing one. Punnoose and Ajit [@punnoose2016] compared seven algorithms and reported that gradient-boosted trees substantially outperformed logistic regression, an ordering that has proved durable: tree ensembles remain the practical default for tabular HR data, for the reasons Breiman set out [@breiman2001] and because class imbalance is tractable with established resampling methods [@chawla2002]. Sarker [@sarker2021] situates this within the broader applied-ML landscape.

The methodological difficulty is that this literature has consolidated around a single dataset. The IBM HR Analytics Employee Attrition dataset [@ibm2017] — 1,470 records, 35 attributes — is the most widely used benchmark in the field, and recent work continues to rely on it exclusively. Li's transformer-based framework [@li2023] and the explainable risk-scoring system of Pavithran and Vadivel [@pavithran2025] both train and evaluate on it alone. Two properties of that dataset deserve more attention than they usually receive: it is **fictional**, generated by IBM data scientists rather than observed, and it describes a single large US employer. A benchmark with those two properties can establish that an algorithm works on data of a certain shape. It cannot establish that a model works on a real workforce, and still less on a workforce in a different economy.

## 2.2 Explainable AI in HR Decision-Making

Where a prediction affects a person's employment, the reasoning behind it becomes material rather than decorative. SHAP [@lundberg2017] provides a unified additive attribution framework grounded in Shapley values, and Lundberg et al. later gave an exact polynomial-time algorithm for tree ensembles [@lundberg2020], removing the approximation error that limits model-agnostic explainers. This matters for the present work: with a tree ensemble, per-prediction attributions are exact rather than sampled.

Applied HR systems have adopted this quickly. Mohiuddin et al. [@mohiuddin2023] combine SHAP attributions with what-if analysis in a decision-support system, so that a manager sees not only which factors drove a risk score but which changes would plausibly alter it — the step from explanation to intervention.

Rudin [@rudin2019] supplies the necessary counterweight. Her argument is that post-hoc explanation of a black box is not equivalent to interpretability, and that in high-stakes domains an inherently interpretable model should be preferred outright, since an explanation that is merely plausible can legitimise a model that is wrong. Employment decisions fall squarely within her definition of high-stakes. The position adopted in this dissertation is a middle one, and is stated as such: a random forest with exact tree SHAP is neither a black box explained by a surrogate nor a genuinely interpretable model, and the explanations it produces are treated throughout as evidence about *the model's behaviour*, not as evidence about why an employee is actually likely to leave.

## 2.3 Cross-Context Transfer of Attrition Models

This section carries the study's central claim, so the argument is set out in three steps: what the ML literature implicitly assumes, what the statistical and organisational literatures predict, and what has actually been tested.

**The assumption.** Attrition studies are routinely written as though the model produced is a general instrument. A system trained and validated on one organisation's records is presented as a method for predicting employee attrition, not as a method for predicting attrition *at that organisation*. The assumption is rarely defended because it is rarely stated. Where authors do acknowledge it, they defer it: Pavithran and Vadivel note that their dataset "might limit the model's capacity to generalize across different types of organizations" and recommend extension to "multi-organizational and industry-specific datasets" as future work [@pavithran2025], while Li's evaluation does not address cross-organisational generalisation at all [@li2023]. The recurring call for validation on diverse geographic and sectoral data is itself evidence that such validation has not generally been performed.

**What theory predicts.** Moreno-Torres et al. [@morenotorres2012] provide the vocabulary for what should be expected when a model is moved between populations, distinguishing covariate shift (the feature distribution changes), prior probability shift (the base rate changes), and concept shift (the relationship between features and outcome changes). Cross-national HR data plausibly exhibits all three: workforce age and tenure structures differ, attrition base rates differ, and — critically — the meaning of the predictors may differ. Concept shift is the damaging case, because it cannot be repaired by reweighting.

That concept shift is not hypothetical here is established outside the ML literature. Fischer and Mansell's cross-cultural meta-analysis [@fischer2009] found that the relationships between forms of organisational commitment and turnover intention vary systematically with national cultural context — affective commitment relating more strongly to turnover intentions in individualistic settings, normative commitment more strongly in collectivistic ones. The organisational behaviour literature therefore predicts that the structural relationship an attrition model learns in one national context will not hold unchanged in another.

**What has been tested.** Very little. The ML literature assumes transfer and defers testing it; the OB literature predicts that transfer should partly fail but does not express this as a predictive-model evaluation. For Sri Lanka specifically, no published study evaluates an internationally-trained attrition model against local data. The gap is thus not an absence of models but an absence of an honest transfer test — and, as §1.2 noted, the deeper obstacle is that no public individual-level record of observed Sri Lankan attrition exists against which such a test could be run on behavioural outcomes.

## 2.4 Serverless Architecture and the Economics of Deployed ML

Machine learning as a service [@ribeiro2015] removed the requirement that an organisation own infrastructure to use a model. Serverless computing extends this by removing the requirement to provision capacity at all: the Berkeley view [@jonas2019] characterises the shift as an abstraction comparable to the move from assembly to high-level languages, with the operational consequence that idle capacity is not billed. Managed platforms package training and serving on the same basis [@googlecloud2024vertex].

The cost evidence is genuinely contested, and the disagreement is informative. Adzic and Chatley [@adzic2017] document two industrial migrations in which moving to a function-based deployment reduced hosting costs substantially. Eivy and Weinman [@eivy2017] argue the opposite tendency, showing that per-invocation pricing can grow rapidly even at modest traffic, that provider free quotas are insufficient for a service of any real size, and that the economics depend heavily on the execution profile of the workload.

Read together, these are not contradictory findings but a statement about workload shape. Serverless deployment is economically favourable where duty cycle is low and traffic is bursty, and unfavourable where load is sustained and predictable. Attrition inference for a fifty-employee firm — a handful of predictions per week, a monthly retrain — sits at the extreme favourable end of that spectrum, which makes it a strong candidate case rather than a general endorsement. Two qualifications follow and are carried into Chapter 5. First, scale-to-zero imposes cold-start latency, an engineering cost incurred in exchange for the economic one. Second, the argument applies only to components that *can* be made stateless; a relational database that must remain available cannot, and its cost is unaffected. Neither the general serverless cost literature nor the HR analytics literature has measured this trade-off for an SME-scale deployment in a low-income economy, where the absolute budget rather than the relative saving is the binding constraint [@charney2024].

## 2.5 HR Analytics in SMEs and Developing Economies

Angrave et al. [@angrave2016] argued that HR was poorly positioned to exploit analytics, citing weak quantitative capability and tools designed around reporting rather than decision-making. Subsequent empirical work has borne this out and refined it for smaller firms. Ilyas et al. [@ilyas2026], surveying 308 HR professionals in SMEs in a developing economy, found adoption driven by performance expectancy, effort expectancy, facilitating conditions and social influence, with perceived trust and supervisor support acting as significant additional determinants — that is, by perceived usability and organisational support rather than by predictive accuracy.

Bahanni et al. [@bahanni2026] interviewed 31 HR managers in Morocco and describe how external constraints — sociocultural norms and underdeveloped technological infrastructure — interact with internal weaknesses in data governance and inconsistent HR practice to produce compounding barriers. The Sri Lankan picture is consistent: Kirupananthan et al. [@kirupananthan2024] report low AI readiness among local SMEs, in an economy where SMEs contribute roughly half of GDP [@dcs2023] and skilled emigration sustains turnover pressure [@slbfe2023].

The design implication is direct, and it shapes this project's contribution. For this population the binding constraints are cost, integration into existing workflow, and trust — not algorithmic sophistication. A more accurate model delivered as a separate tool at enterprise pricing does not address the barrier that the evidence identifies.

### 2.5.1 Evaluating Usability with Small or No Samples

Since usability rather than accuracy governs adoption in this setting, its measurement matters. The System Usability Scale [@brooke1996] is the standard instrument, and Bangor et al. [@bangor2009] supply the interpretive bands that make a raw SUS score meaningful by attaching adjective ratings to score ranges. Both require participants. Where participants cannot be recruited, Nielsen and Molich's heuristic evaluation [@nielsen1990] offers an expert-inspection alternative in which a small number of evaluators assess an interface against a fixed set of usability principles. The two methods are not equivalent — heuristic evaluation identifies plausible problems, whereas SUS measures experienced satisfaction — and Chapter 5 states explicitly what the substitution made in this study can and cannot establish.

## 2.6 Turnover Intention and Psychometric Constructs

The Sri Lankan validation data used in this study measures turnover *intention* rather than observed departure, so the standing of intention as a construct requires justification rather than apology.

Mobley [@mobley1977] modelled withdrawal as a sequence — dissatisfaction, thoughts of quitting, evaluation of alternatives, intention to search, intention to quit, departure — in which intention is the proximal antecedent of the behaviour. Ajzen's theory of planned behaviour [@ajzen1991] generalises this: intention is the immediate determinant of volitional action. The empirical warrant is provided by Steel and Ovalle's meta-analysis [@steel1984], which reports a weighted average correlation of approximately **0.50** between behavioural intentions and actual turnover, and finds intention to be a better predictor of leaving than either job satisfaction or organisational commitment.

That figure is the honest basis for the proxy and also its limit. An association of 0.50 is the strongest available among the standard antecedents, which makes intention a defensible target; it also implies roughly a quarter of shared variance, which means intention is emphatically not a substitute for the observed outcome. Every claim in this dissertation about the local model is therefore a claim about intention, and is worded that way.

The specific instrument used here is that of Kanchana and Jayathilaka [@kanchana2023], who surveyed professionals in Sri Lankan startups across eight psychometric constructs — job satisfaction, work–life balance, happiness, management support, career management, innovative work behaviour, leader–member exchange and coworker support — with turnover intention as the outcome. Because predictors and outcome are captured in a single self-report instrument, common method variance is a structural feature of the design rather than an incidental risk; Podsakoff et al. [@podsakoff2003] set out both the mechanism and the available remedies, and §5.4 reports what could and could not be ruled out here.

## 2.7 Fairness and Ethics in Algorithmic HR

Barocas and Selbst [@barocas2016] established the foundational point that discriminatory outcomes in data-driven decision systems arise from the data and the target definition rather than from discriminatory intent, and that existing anti-discrimination doctrine maps awkwardly onto them. Raghavan et al. [@raghavan2020] examined 18 vendors of algorithmic pre-employment assessments and found bias-mitigation claims that were inconsistently specified and rarely independently verifiable — evidence that the gap between stated and demonstrated fairness in commercial HR AI is wide.

The formal results constrain what any such system can promise. Kleinberg et al. [@kleinberg2016] and Chouldechova [@chouldechova2017] independently proved that calibration and equalised error rates across groups cannot generally be satisfied simultaneously when base rates differ. A deployed system must therefore choose which fairness criterion it satisfies and disclose the choice; claiming to be fair without qualification is not available.

For this project the operative requirements are the four-fifths rule as a disparate-impact screen, and Sri Lanka's Personal Data Protection Act No. 9 of 2022 [@pdpa2022], which governs the lawful basis, minimisation and security of the personal data involved. §5.8 reports the fairness audit and, importantly, the point at which the available sample was too small to support one.

## 2.8 Synthesis and Research Gap

Four gaps emerge, and together they define the study.

**Transfer is assumed, not tested.** The attrition literature consolidates on a single fictional US dataset and defers cross-context validation to future work, while the cross-cultural organisational literature predicts that the learned relationships should not hold unchanged. No study evaluates an internationally-trained attrition model against Sri Lankan data.

**Serverless economics are argued, not measured, at SME scale.** The cost literature disagrees in a way that turns on workload shape, and low-duty-cycle HR inference is exactly the case not yet measured — least of all against an absolute budget in a low-income economy.

**Adoption research and modelling research do not meet.** Studies of SME adoption identify cost, integration and trust as the barriers; modelling studies optimise accuracy on a benchmark. Little work delivers a model inside the system where HR work actually happens, at a cost the target firms could pay.

**The data required does not exist.** No public individual-level record of observed Sri Lankan attrition is available, which is why the alternatives this study evaluates — international transfer and calibrated synthetic augmentation — are the ones a resource-constrained developer would actually reach for, and why establishing whether they work is worth doing.

Chapter 3 sets out the methodology through which these gaps are addressed.
